Young ethical hackers: digital safety and care

Curiosity, respect, and protection: learn to make safer choices online and when to ask for help.

An ethical person explores only what belongs to them or what they have clear permission to use. Curiosity is not permission.

Our safety agreement

Take this course with a responsible adult. Activities use stories, cards, and drawings: do not access other people’s accounts, websites, networks, or devices; do not install tools; and never share passwords or personal information. If something feels wrong, stop and talk to a trusted adult.

About this course

Provisional audience
Children ages 9–12, supported by an adult. The age range and language still need educational review.
Prerequisites
None. An adult can read the activities aloud.
Materials
Paper and pencil; no account, installation, or required cost.
Learning goals
Explain permission and privacy; recognize personal information; invent a passphrase without revealing it; spot fictional suspicious messages; and know when to ask for help.
Assessment
Explain a safe choice, recognize when permission is missing, and name someone to ask for help. No intrusion challenge.
Status
Editorial pilot. An educator and child-safeguarding reviewer must review it before it is considered a final course.

Six short activities

  1. Curiosity and permission

    In an imaginary story, decide what a character may do with their own toy and what requires the owner’s permission. Rule: explore only what is yours or what you have clear permission to use.

  2. Personal information

    Use made-up cards to sort information that can identify someone—such as an address, password, or location—from information that is safer to share. Do not use real information about the child or family.

  3. Passphrase

    Write a long phrase for an imaginary character on paper. Never write, say, photograph, or send anyone’s real password or the activity.

  4. Suspicious message

    Read a fictional example prepared by the adult. Look for urgency, requests for secrecy, or requests for information. Do not click or reply: stop and check with a responsible adult using a known contact method.

  5. Make-believe mission

    Draw an imaginary house and mark three safety habits: a closed door, a safely kept key, and a confirmed visitor. Connect each habit to a digital safety idea without accessing networks or devices.

  6. My safety promise

    Make a poster with three steps: “I stopped,” “I did not share,” and “I asked for help.” Tell an adult which rule you want to remember.

How to notice learning

  • With support: recognizes private information and talks about asking for help.
  • Age-appropriate independence: explains why passwords and personal data should not be shared and recognizes when permission is needed.
  • Next step: helps the family make a safety agreement, with adult guidance.

Review and limits

This introductory material is not penetration-testing training, does not qualify anyone to conduct audits or work professionally, and does not replace adult supervision. It remains a pilot until the reviews and sources below are recorded.
